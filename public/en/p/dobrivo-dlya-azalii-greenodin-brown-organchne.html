The indoor azalea is a species of the genus Rhododendron, related to the tall garden rhododendrons. There are many species in the wild, but only a few, along with their hybrids, are grown at home. The azalea is a houseplant that forms a low shrub 30 to 50 cm tall and is known for its lush, beautiful flowers. Azalea flowers can be red, white, pink, lilac or bicoloured, which makes the plant very popular with houseplant lovers. One of its main advantages is that it blooms in the cold season, when many other plants are dormant.

Types and varieties of indoor azalea

When people talk about the variety of azaleas, they usually mean species known only to specialists. In shops you will mostly find hybrid azaleas sold as “White azalea”, “Pink azalea”, “Red azalea”, “Azalea mix” or “Azalea indica”.

Varieties differ in flowering time, from mid- or late winter to early spring.

There are two main types of indoor azalea. The Indian azalea, or Sims's rhododendron (Rhododendron simsii), is the most popular with houseplant lovers. It is an evergreen shrub with small dark green leaves and reddish down. The flowers can be double or single, usually 3–5 cm or more across, and grow in rounded clusters. The Japanese azalea, or Kurume azalea (Rhododendron obtusum), has smaller flowers and a more compact habit than the Indian type and can be grown both indoors and in the garden.

Azalea care

Azaleas need a special approach, as in nature they are used to specific conditions such as a cool mountain climate and a particular type of soil.

Soil

For optimal growth and abundant flowering, the plant needs acidic soil with a pH of 3.5 to 5.5. Neutral or alkaline soils can cause disease and even kill the plant. It is best to use ready-made potting mixes designed for azaleas, rhododendrons, camellias and heathers.

GREENODIN BROWN organo-mineral granular fertilizer (pH 5.0) with beneficial bacteria restores soil fertility and helps the plant draw all the trace elements it needs from the soil.

Humidity

To keep the air humid enough, mist the azalea regularly. Take special care during flowering, as water droplets can damage the flowers and make them go dark. Use a fine mister, place containers of water nearby or stand the pot on a tray filled with a moist material such as sphagnum moss, expanded clay or gravel.

Watering

Indoor azalea care can be tricky because of watering. Too little water dries out the root ball, while too much leads to root rot. Keep the soil slightly moist at all times, especially during flowering, when the plant needs more water. Remember to empty the saucer after watering.

GREENODIN BROWN azalea fertilizer helps with this careful routine thanks to its water-retaining properties.

One way to keep moisture levels right is to stand the pot in a bowl of cool water in hot weather or when the soil dries out. Some experts recommend combining bottom watering with regular watering all year round. You can also place ice cubes on the soil, which has a cooling effect on the plant when it is hot.

Water azaleas with soft water that has stood for at least 2–3 days. Tap water contains impurities that can harm the plant. Lime is especially dangerous because it reduces soil acidity, and the plant also reacts badly to chlorine. The recommended fertilizer neutralises these problems.

To maintain the right soil pH and water-holding capacity, promote abundant flowering and increase stress resistance, we recommend GREENODIN BROWN organo-mineral granular fertilizer.

Light

Indoor azaleas do best in bright, indirect light that lets them grow and develop. Direct sun is harmful. Azaleas can be grown in partial shade, but in winter they need extra light from a grow lamp while setting buds and flowering.

Temperature

One of the challenges of growing indoor azaleas is getting the temperature right. They dislike warmth: the ideal temperature outside flowering is 15–18 °C, and abundant flowering can be achieved by lowering it to 10–12 °C while buds are forming.

Pruning

To shape an ideal indoor azalea bush, prune regularly and carefully. After flowering, always remove spent flower clusters and dry or weak shoots, and cut shoots back by a few centimetres. From late spring to early summer, the plant puts out young shoots, and their tips should be pinched out. Continue this throughout the season until buds appear. Also remove young shoots that appear next to flower buds. To prolong flowering, remove faded azalea flowers.

Feeding indoor azaleas

In spring and summer, apply GREENODIN BROWN organic azalea fertilizer when watering: 25 g (one teaspoon) per 1 L of potting mix (mixed in) or 25 g per 1 m² (scattered).

In winter, feed with GREENODIN BROWN at the same rate.

Propagating indoor azaleas

Propagating indoor azaleas is not difficult, but it does not always succeed. Semi-ripe cuttings are used. In summer, take cuttings from young shoots that are at least six months old and have just started to turn woody. GREENODIN products create ideal conditions for indoor azaleas, so propagation takes place in ideal conditions too.

A cutting should be 7–10 cm long with several pairs of leaves. Cut it at an angle with a clean, sharp knife and remove the leaves, leaving only the top few. Plant the lower end 2–3 cm deep in azalea compost or a 1:1 mix of sand and peat. Water the substrate well, cover the container with clear material and keep it in a warm place at 24–25 °C. Air the cuttings every few days.

The soil should be moist but not wet. If flower buds appear on the cuttings, remove them.

Rooting usually takes 1.5–2 months. One sign that it is complete is the appearance of young shoots. Once rooted, the cover is removed and the cuttings are grown on a little longer before being potted up individually.

Indoor azaleas can also be propagated by division, but this is risky because the root system is very fragile and sensitive to mechanical damage.

Repotting indoor azaleas

Repot indoor azaleas only during the dormant period after flowering. Young plants should be repotted every year, and once they are 3–4 years old, every three years or even less often.

Repotting is needed when roots start coming out of the drainage holes or show on the soil surface. Do it with great care, as the roots are shallow and easily damaged. Avoid breaking up the root ball and do not untangle the roots.

Choose a pot slightly larger than the old one (by 2–3 cm) with a drainage layer at the bottom. Set the root collar at the same level as before. Add soil very gently, without pressing or firming it.

Water well after repotting and top up with soil if it settles.

Indoor azalea diseases

Like any plant, azaleas are prone to various diseases. Here are the main ones and how to deal with them.

Root rot

Root rot is a common problem caused by too much moisture and standing water in the pot. Leaves wilt all over the plant, and stems go limp and may droop or flop over. To confirm the cause, take the plant out of its pot: you will see blackened, slimy roots that come away easily.

To save the plant, remove the rotten roots, treat the root system with potassium permanganate solution or a fungicide and repot it in fresh soil. Even so, an azalea's chances of surviving root rot are slim.

Grey mould

Grey mould shows up as rotting spots on leaves and shoots. Infected tissue softens and dies, and eventually the plant dies too. The main causes are incorrect care and watering.

To fight the disease, remove the rotten parts of the plant and let the soil in the pot dry out. Treat the plant and soil with fungicides that contain no chlorine or lime.

Phytophthora blight

Phytophthora blight is a dangerous and often incurable disease of indoor azaleas. The first signs are brown spots on the leaves. If no action is taken, the spots grow and parts of the plant start to die.

Curing the plant is very difficult and rarely succeeds.

To treat it, remove the affected parts, treat the plant with suitable products and repot it in fresh soil.

Chlorosis

Chlorosis is particularly harmful to leaves because it reduces chlorophyll production and photosynthesis. This can have serious consequences for the plant and its growth.
